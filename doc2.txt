Recent progress in general-domain Vision Language Models (VLMs) has enabled increasingly strong temporal
reasoning over extended video streams. However, the surgical AI community has so far lacked a dedicated,
standardized challenge that evaluates whether these emerging capabili.es translate to real clinical workflows, where
cri.cal events unfold over tens of minutes to hours. This gap is important: many clinically meaningful ques.ons in
minimally invasive surgery require persistent memory, temporal consistency, and reasoning across long .me horizons.

The ORena SAVE FOCUS challenge addresses this unmet need by providing a structured benchmark that targets an
urgent pa.ent-safety problem in minimally invasive procedures: ensuring the retrieval of foreign objects, such as
sponges and needles, from the abdomen at the end of the opera.on. Uninten.onally leaving foreign objects in the
abdomen is a rare but consequen.al incident in minimally invasive surgery, as they can cause serious complica.ons.

Specifically, FOCUS aims to generate scien.fic progress by tackling two fundamental research ques.ons (RQs):

RQ1 (Clinical u.lity): Can VLMs generate clinically meaningful and safety-relevant informa.on about surgical foreign
objects? This ques.on targets the clinical value of VLMs for intraopera.ve quality assurance, focusing on ac.onable
insights related to foreign objects such as sponges, needles, and clips.

RQ2 (Technical limits): What are the current limita.ons of VLMs in surgical scene reasoning? To answer this ques.on,
FOCUS encompasses three tracks that progressively increase the temporal and contextual demands on the model: a
FRAME Track (single-image understanding) to assess founda.onal visual percep.on and surgical-domain
interpreta.on; a SEGMENT Track (short video segments) to evaluate short-term temporal reasoning, local tracking, and
ac.on understanding; and a PROCEDURE Track (long-context up to full procedures reflec.ng real intraopera.ve and
postopera.ve query scenarios) to probe long-horizon memory, persistent object tracking across occlusions and scene
changes, aggrega.on over .me (e.g., coun.ng and retrieval status), and global reasoning across events. Together,
these tracks enable a systema.c characteriza.on of where current VLMs succeed and fail as task complexity transi.ons
from instantaneous percep.on to long-context intraopera.ve reasoning.
Cri.cally, FOCUS is enabled by a unique dataset that, to the best of our knowledge, is the first challenge resource to
provide full-length laparoscopic videos with fine-grained foreign-object annota.ons at scale. Importantly, the mul.-
center dataset includes instance-consistent labels, making it possible to evaluate models not only on short-term
detec.on, but also on long-horizon tracking, coun.ng, and retrieval verifica.on across extended procedures. With a
total of over 100,000 Visual Ques.on Answering (VQA) pairs obtained from 400 surgical videos acquired from all over
the world, FOCUS establishes a standardized benchmark that simultaneously (i) probes the technical limits of long-
context VLMs in real-world surgical video and (ii) addresses a concrete quality-assurance objec.ve with direct
relevance to intraopera.ve pa.ent safety.

Novelty of the challenge

FOCUS is the first surgical VLM challenge to explicitly benchmark long-context, understanding of full-length
laparoscopic procedures, rather than only short, curated segments. It introduces a structured evalua.on framework

Task descrip=on and applica=on scenarios

Briefly describe the applica.on scenarios for the tasks in the challenge.

In minimally invasive surgical procedures, surgical sponges, sutures and other foreign objects are inserted into the
abdomen via trocar sites to carry out steps of the procedure. These objects frequently remain intra-abdominally for
extended periods of the procedure, as objects, such as sponges, may contribute to exposure of the surgical field or
may be reused. Uninten.onally leaving foreign objects in the abdomen is a rare but consequen.al incident in
minimally invasive surgery, as they can cause serious complica.ons [1]. To avoid such incidents, coun.ng of all foreign
objects at the beginning and the end of every procedure has been established as a standard quality control measure to
ensure that all used objects are removed from the abdomen by the end of the procedure. However, due to the
con.nuously changing and closed-up view in laparoscopic surgery, localizing remaining objects intra-abdominally at
the end of surgical procedures can be .me-consuming and represents a prac.cal nuisance that surgeons rou.nely
encounter.

The challenge tasks reflect intraopera.ve quality-assurance scenarios in minimally invasive surgery where surgeons
must ensure foreign objects are safely managed. Example applica.ons include con.nuous coun.ng and tracking of
sponges, needles, clips, sutures, and specimen bags, real-.me localiza.on of the last known posi.on of an object that
is unaccounted for, and verifica.on that all introduced items requiring removal have been retrieved before closure.

FOCUS benchmarks vision­language models on clinically relevant Visual Ques.on Answering (VQA) tasks for foreign
object understanding in minimally invasive surgery, with the goal of advancing AI methods that can support
intraopera.ve quality assurance and pa.ent safety. In this context, VQA refers to the task of answering structured,
clinically grounded ques.ons based on surgical video content and associated metadata.

In the context of this challenge, a foreign object refers to an object that is fully introduced into the pa.ent's body
cavity during a surgical procedure and is expected to be retrieved or accounted for. This includes items such as surgical
sponges, compresses, needles, clips, drains, specimen bags, elas.c straps, and similar objects that are temporarily
placed intra-abdominally.
In addi.on, isolated biological material that is detached during the procedure and must be retrieved - such as an
excised appendix, a gallstone, or resected .ssue fragments - are also considered foreign objects under this defini.on,
as they require removal from the body cavity before comple.on of the opera.on.

Importantly, standard surgical instruments that remain connected to the external environment (e.g., graspers, scissors,
trocars, staplers, cameras) are not considered foreign objects. Furthermore, we exclude detachable parts of surgical
instruments, par.cularly anvil components of staplers.

FOCUS is organized in three tasks, here referred to as tracks.

The FRAME Track evaluates a model's ability to answer clinically relevant ques.ons from a single image. This track
targets core surgical scene understanding skills such as foreign object iden.fica.on, ajribute recogni.on, and spa.al
localiza.on within a single moment in .me. It serves as an accessible entry point while establishing a strong baseline
for visual percep.on and surgical-domain image interpreta.on.

The SEGMENT Track focuses on short video segments (up to 5 min), requiring models to incorporate local temporal
context to answer ques.ons about foreign objects and their interac.ons with anatomy and instruments. Tasks
emphasize mo.on-aware percep.on, short-term tracking, and understanding of brief ac.on sequences, such as
inser.on, manipula.on, or removal of sponges, needles, or clips. This track bridges sta.c recogni.on and longer-
horizon reasoning by tes.ng whether models can integrate events across .me within a bounded context.

The PROCEDURE Track challenges models with long videos (procedures from beginning up to a certain point reflec.ng
real intraopera.ve and postopera.ve query scenarios), to assess their capacity for long-term memory, persistent
tracking, and global reasoning. Ques.ons may require recalling when an object was last seen, aggrega.ng object
counts across long intervals, or determining whether all introduced foreign objects requiring retrieval have been
removed. This track targets the key technical fron.er of long-context VLMs and directly reflects real-world
intraopera.ve quality assurance needs, where cri.cal safety ques.ons depend on understanding events that occurred
much earlier in the opera.on.

Method used to compute a performance rank

Describe the method used to compute a performance rank for all submijed algorithms based on the generated metric
results on the test cases. Typically the text will describe how results obtained per case and metric are aggregated to
arrive at a final score/ranking. Ideally, provide the ranking scheme as a concrete pseudo code.

Key design goals

     · Interpretability: while we take a complex approach to the detailed performance analysis of methods (see
          sta.s.cs sec.on below), our aim was to have an easy-to-understand ranking scheme. For this reason, we
          based the ranking on a single metric.

     · Fairness across categories: avoid bias from uneven ques.on counts (e.g., many iden.fica.on ques.ons)
     · Noise resistance: irrelevant performance differences within a category should not be rewarded
     · Robustness-first: emphasize generaliza.on, e.g., procedure types that have not been present in the training

          data
     · Theore.cal guarantees: although no merging mechanism can check all boxes of desirable proper.es ("Arrow's

          impossibility theorem"), we prefer one with overall more desirable proper.es
     · Reproducibility: determinis.c and transparent aggrega.on rules

Overview

The ranking happens in three steps:

     1. Capability-level ranking: For different capabili.es individual rankings are created. Irrelevant performance
          differences result in iden.cal ranks.

     2. Merging through Copeland method: The individual rankings are combined to determine the overall ranking.
          We use the Copeland method to do so [1].

     3. Resolving .es: For the top 3 places we will resolve .es by looking at the mean win rate of .ed models using
          bootstrapping within all capabili.es.
Proposed ranking scheme

Each test case is annotated with the following meta informa.on:

   i. Robustness level: In-distribu.on (ID) vs Out-of-distribu.on (OOD) tag with respect to procedure type and
          ques.on

   ii. Primary capability: Given the primary ques.on intent, the test case is mapped to one primary capability using
          the proposed taxonomy (Main capabili.es: Object Recogni.on and Instance Matching, Temporal Grounding,
          Aggrega.on, Event and Scene Understanding, Reasoning)

Using these ajributes we split all ques.ons into buckets: For each model, and each primary category, model scores are
computed for the ID and OOD ques.ons. They represent the mean Accuracy over all ques.ons belonging to the
respec.ve primary category and distribu.on split.

The ini.al ranking for each bucket is based on this Accuracy score. Then we will perform significance tests between
pairs of models to determine whether a performance delta truly jus.fies different ranks and adjust the ranking so that
non-significant differences result in the same rank posi.on.

The merged ranking is then computed from the Copeland rule over all rankings. We say model A dominates model B in
case A is ranked higher than B more omen than B is ranked higher than A. The score of each model is given by the
number of models it dominates minus the number of models it is dominated by. The higher the score, the bejer.

In case of .es within the first three posi.ons, we will resolve them by directly comparing all .ed models. Within each
bucket we perform bootstrapping and compute the respec.ve Accuracies of any bootstrap sample. The win rate is the
frac.on of bootstrap samples that result in a model to have the highest Accuracy of all models. Tied models are then
sorted by their mean win rate over all buckets.

To outperform a baseline model during the pre-evalua.on phase it suffices to have a higher mean Accuracy over the
buckets. We will also make mean accuracy performances of the final phase public.
